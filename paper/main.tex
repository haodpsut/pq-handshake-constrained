\begin{abstract}
EDHOC is deployed on constrained links because it is smaller than DTLS, and measurements on
IEEE~802.15.4 hardware confirm the size reduction while showing it yields a much smaller gain
in handshake duration. We ask what remains of that argument once credentials become
post-quantum. An EDHOC message then no longer fits inside one 802.15.4 frame, so CoAP
block-wise transfer engages and the handshake becomes a sequence of acknowledged blocks, while
DTLS holds its round-trip count fixed over the same range, which we measure rather than cite.
Counted in direction changes on both protocols, the penalty is \numRatioDefault$\times$ at the
shipped block size and \numRatioBest$\times$ at the largest RFC~7959 permits, which neither
constrained stack can select; modelling RFC~9177 leaves \numRatioQBlock$\times$ and no
constrained stack implements it. On airtime the same settings give
\numRatioFramesDefault$\times$ and \numRatioFrames$\times$, so the cost is dominated by the
lock-step structure rather than by bytes sent. Separately, with classical credentials alone,
three widely used DTLS libraries prove bounded by three different quantities, one unable to
complete a handshake at an 802.15.4 frame payload at any credential size. Code and measurements
are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}

The case for EDHOC on constrained links is a size argument, and post-quantum credentials move
the handshake across a size threshold rather than along a scale. What follows is a transport
change, not a larger version of the same cost: block-wise transfer engages in message\_1 at
every standardised parameter set, because a KEM public key has to be transmitted and the
smallest standardised one already exceeds the frame.

How much that costs depends on a shipped default rather than on the protocol. At the block
size both constrained stacks ship, and which RIOT enforces at compile time, the penalty
against a measured DTLS baseline is large; at the largest block size RFC~7959 permits it is
small. Neither stack can reach the latter, and the alternative transfer options of RFC~9177,
which would recover most of the difference, are implemented by neither and referenced by
neither EDHOC document.

The implementation survey points the other way from what motivated it. Of three libraries, the
one actually deployed on constrained devices is the one that handles the constrained link;
the two server-side implementations do not, and they fail for unrelated reasons. Anyone
comparing EDHOC against ``DTLS'' on such a link is comparing against whichever library they
picked, in a version none of them offers over datagrams.

Mitigations exist and do not change this. Handshake caching removes repeated handshakes
altogether~\cite{bodenhausen2025}, hardware acceleration and unified post-quantum
architectures reduce the computational half~\cite{batmaz2024,dobias2025}, and profiles such as
the DTLS profile for ACE narrow what is exchanged~\cite{rfc9430}. None alters whether a
message fits inside a frame.

What is not claimed: nothing here measures latency, energy, or memory, all measured on real
hardware by others, and nothing supports a statement about DTLS as a protocol rather than
about three implementations of it.
