\title{Does the Lightweight Handshake Stay Lightweight?\\
EDHOC, DTLS~1.3 and Post-Quantum Credentials on IEEE~802.15.4 Links}
\author{(authors withheld for now)}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
EDHOC is deployed on constrained links because it is smaller than DTLS~1.3, and measurements
on IEEE~802.15.4 hardware confirm the size reduction while also showing that it translates
into a considerably smaller gain in handshake duration. We ask what remains of the argument
once credentials become post-quantum. Because an EDHOC message then no longer fits inside one
802.15.4 frame, CoAP block-wise transfer engages and the handshake becomes a sequence of
acknowledged blocks, while DTLS fragments at its own handshake layer and keeps its round-trip
count fixed; the EDHOC-over-CoAP profile itself makes its two-round-trip minimum conditional on
exactly the size condition that post-quantum keys break. We validate an exchange-count model
against an independent CoAP implementation, and sweep the full range of permitted block sizes
to show that no setting recovers the round trips. Separately, and with classical credentials
alone, we find that three widely used DTLS libraries are bounded by three different quantities
on such a link, one of them unable to complete a handshake at an 802.15.4 frame payload at any
credential size. Code and measurements are released.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
EDHOC, DTLS, post-quantum cryptography, CoAP, IEEE 802.15.4, constrained devices.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Conclusion}

The case for EDHOC on constrained links is a size argument, and the measurement that supports
it also shows the advantage translating only partly into duration. Post-quantum credentials
move the handshake across a size threshold rather than along a scale: block-wise transfer
engages in message\_1 at every standardised parameter set, and no permitted block size brings
the exchange count near the flight count DTLS holds constant. The threshold follows from
having to transmit a KEM public key, so it is not something a different EDHOC profile can
design around.

The implementation survey points the other way from what motivated it. Of the three libraries
examined, the one actually deployed on constrained devices is the one that handles the
constrained link; the two server-side implementations do not, and they fail for unrelated
reasons, one at a minimum MTU and one at a fragment count. Anyone comparing EDHOC against
``DTLS'' on such a link is comparing against whichever library they picked.

What is not claimed: nothing here measures latency, energy, or memory, all of which have been
measured on real hardware by others, and nothing here supports a statement about DTLS as a
protocol rather than about three implementations of it.
